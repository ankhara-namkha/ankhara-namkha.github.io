The 2-minute version

  • Not a ladder. You don't "graduate" from earlier training. Meditation stays the thread, and the emphasis widens gradually from working on ourselves toward the world and the idea of enlightened society. 📖 SP ch.1 "Creating an Enlightened Society"; SP ch.2 "Discovering Basic Goodness"
  • The main text is a poem about the fearful mind. You don't get rid of fear. You put it "in the cradle of loving-kindness", nourish it with the "milk of eternal doubtlessness", cool and fan it, and lead it to the "self-existing playground" of experience. 📖 SP ch.10 "Letting Go"
  • The "archery range of the warriors": act without clinging to the result. Every action brings a reply from the world, and when the reply surprises you, your assumptions crack open and reality "opens up." 📖 SP ch.10 "Letting Go"
  • A warrior is simply a brave person. That includes the bravery of being gentle and of looking at your own mind. 📖 GES Prologue ("A warrior, or pawo in Tibetan, is a brave person")

Part 1 — Not a ladder: stepping into the sacred path (00:08–46:50) · about 6 min read

⏭️ Skip [00:08–11:36]: Recording check, bow, and round-the-room introductions. Nothing taught.

11:40 Why this isn't a "graduate program"
  • The teacher is trying not to say "level." It has been in her vocabulary for 40 years, but she no longer thinks of this as a ladder.
  • She is also trying not to say "graduate program." That phrase suggests you have finished something and are moving on to higher study. That is academic language, and it does not fit here.
  • What carries on from the earlier warriorship training is meditation, the key to everything that follows. You keep honing those skills.

The inner and outer words. The teacher looked at the title of the book: Shambhala: The Sacred Path of the Warrior. - "Shambhala warrior" are the outer words. That is what the early warriorship training introduces. - "The sacred path" are the inner words. - So this transition is a move to a more inner level of the Shambhala teachings as a whole. It is not a personal inner level.

The first volume was like a guidebook to Shambhala or a road map of the warrior’s path. Great Eastern Sun is about transmission and about embodying and manifesting.

— GES front matter (Preface) ¶367

📖 In the books: GES front matter (Preface); GES Prologue; GES ch.2 "Working with Early Morning Depression" (sitting practice as the main vehicle)

16:46 What the warrior's outlook is
  • The sacred path topics are Trungpa Rinpoche's teachings, many of them given at major seminars. His students later arranged them into a series, and that arrangement has been reshuffled over the years. So the particular design is not the key point; the teachings themselves are.
  • The early warriorship training introduces the warrior as someone who shows gentleness, fearlessness and intelligence.
  • The sacred path gives the warrior's outlook (how the warrior sees things) and how that outlook shows up in the world.
  • The Great Eastern Sun is an introduction to that way of seeing. It is a vision with some inner depth to it.

When we talk about becoming a warrior, we are not talking about conducting warfare, but we’re talking about manifesting fearlessness and gentleness that can save the world.

— GES ch.18 "A Question of Heart" ¶623

📖 In the books: GES ch.18 "A Question of Heart"; SP ch.21 "The Shambhala Lineage" (intelligence binding gentleness and fearlessness); GES Epilogue (source of the seminar material)

20:07 The Letter of the Black Ashe: arrogance is not part of the warrior's view
  • There is a different way of thinking from our conventional one. It shows up in a Shambhala text called The Letter of the Black Ashe, parts of which are quoted in Shambhala: The Sacred Path of the Warrior.
  • Its statements are flat: the warrior always does this, the warrior never does that.
  • The teacher's first reaction was "okay, count me out." Examples: the warrior never enters the lower realms, because the warrior rests in trust and modesty. Warriors are never bloated with the poison of arrogance.
  • Her point is that these are not moralistic. She is not claiming she has never been arrogant. The claim is that arrogance is not part of the warrior's perspective. If I am arrogant, I am simply not being a warrior in that moment.
  • It took her a while to "grok" this (she jokes about dating herself with the word, from Stranger in a Strange Land). We gradually learn another way of looking.

Humility, very simply, is the absence of arrogance.

— GES ch.5 "Discipline in the Four Seasons" ¶451

📖 In the books: SP ch.20 "Authentic Presence"; GES ch.5 "Discipline in the Four Seasons"; Collected Works Vol. 8 (names the Letter of the Black Ashe as a Shambhala terma text)

22:33 From working on ourselves to a bigger view
  • So far, the teacher guesses, meditation has been about us: saving ourselves, de-stressing, dealing with our state of mind.
  • The sacred path shifts to a more universal perspective: who we are, what the world is, and ultimately the whole world and the idea of an enlightened society.
  • She compares it to the bodhisattva aim of benefiting all beings and leaving no one behind. That can sound daunting ("How am I supposed to save all sentient beings? I don't even know them all").
  • Her answer is that the point is that it is broadening. It takes us beyond "me, me, me and how I'm feeling." She calls it the great-grandparent perspective: thinking of those who come long after us.
  • The shift is not like flipping a switch. It is a gradual process of accessing the warrior qualities we already have. Meditation is still the key, but the motivation is a little different.

If we don’t help, nobody will. It is our turn to help the world.

— SP ch.1 "Creating an Enlightened Society" ¶27

📖 In the books: SP ch.1 "Creating an Enlightened Society"; GES ch.4 "The Cosmic Sneeze"; GES ch.9 "How to Cultivate the Great Eastern Sun"; GES ch.16 "Helping Others"

25:40 Your own practice
  • The teachers hope you have a personal practice, without making it feel like a test.
  • How long, what time of day and how often are personal. That you practice is what matters.
  • Fitting practice into the day takes experiment: morning sittings may keep getting crowded by the day's tasks, and a different time may work better. You find your own way.
  • When time is really short, ten or fifteen minutes is fine. The teacher says an hour used to be the standard at the center, while 35 minutes turned out to be her own best length. In recent years the Sakyong has said ten minutes is okay, and she has learned that on a crowded day you can simply give yourself fifteen.
  • Her own example: it took her years to find her best time. Morning or early afternoon kept filling with the day's obligations, and her mind ran on them throughout the sitting. Late afternoon, once her tasks were done and before she turned to cooking dinner, turned out to be best. She knows someone who rises at five to practice, and that would never suit her. Foundation practice (ngöndro) was the exception, when long hours were required.

📖 In the books: Mindfulness in Action (Trungpa: "If you can only set aside ten or fifteen minutes a day to sit, start with that"); GES ch.2 "Working with Early Morning Depression"

Try this: If you have no regular time yet, try a different time of day for a week and see when the day's to-do list stops interrupting you.

28:51 The five parts of the early training as a toolbox

The teacher is not fond of the "toolbox" image, but it captures that each part has a use. She wants you to be conscious of which technique you're using. Her comments on a few of them:

  • Taking relief and uplift: the first part is a wonderful way to begin when you arrive from a busy day with little time to decompress. Sometimes three breaths get you there. Sometimes a short session is spent entirely on decompressing, and that is fine.
  • Meeting yourself honestly: a gentle coming and going, simply meeting yourself kindly, and a way of asking "where am I really at?" When busy or stressed we lose track of our state of mind. At the least this lets you be clear about what's going on, rather than "faking it till you make it."
  • Meeting the world accurately: noticing your environment as it is. If you lay your own trip on the world, you're just painting it another color. It's not a ladder, so it doesn't matter where you are. The teacher says this was the style she was most comfortable in for many years. At times she raised her gaze, found her mind got very busy, and lowered it again.
  • Noticing reactions (the awakened heart): when she raised the gaze, her mind was very busy, and what was awakening in her heart was allowing space for that. It means allowing space and noticing what comes up. Challenging at first, but it gives a little more space in the mind and some choice in action. You notice irritation and can say, "Irritating. Big deal, but no question, irritating," instead of letting it slide into anger.
  • Beyond technique: just the ability to let yourself simply be. It's not easy and can't be faked. Sometimes it helps to just be in your world as it is. She has heard that "just sit and be" was the first instruction Trungpa Rinpoche gave some students, and that they found it impossible and wanted techniques.

First, just be—be a person—and then be a person to others.

— GES ch.1 "A Dot in the Open Sky" ¶397

Part 2 — A guided sitting meditation and a movement practice (47:59–01:26:38) · about 6 min read

47:59 Why we sit, and how to sit
  • Shambhala meditation is done with a view of basic goodness (the idea that you are fundamentally worthwhile and okay). The teacher says some people meditate in a sauna, in the woods or walking on a beach. Here, we meditate in basic goodness, but we are sitting it.
  • Posture is the first thing. It is called meditative posture because the breath is unobstructed in it.
  • Her image is a flower. Your pelvis is the pot, solid on the ground. Your spine is the stem rising from it.
  • A small inward curve in the lower back lets the spine move up. She says her organs feel they have enough room like this.

By meditation here we mean something very basic and simple that is not tied to any one culture. We are talking about a very basic act: sitting on the ground, assuming a good posture, and developing a sense of our spot, our place on this earth. This is the means of rediscovering ourselves and our basic goodness, the means to tune ourselves in to genuine reality, without any expectations or preconceptions.

— SP ch.2 "Discovering Basic Goodness" ¶37

📖 In the books: SP ch.2 "Discovering Basic Goodness" (sitting, posture, upright back, "good sense of head and shoulders"); GES ch.1 "A Dot in the Open Sky" (synchronize mind and body, good posture, keep it simple)

Try this: Sit so the pelvis is heavy on the seat, with a slight lower-back curve and an upright spine.

50:16 Placing attention on the out-breath

🎥 Worth watching [50:16–01:06:30], about 16 min, optional "practice along, audio only": The sit runs mostly in silence with a few spoken cues, and doing it with her voice is easier than reading the steps. Audio is enough, no video needed.

  1. Take the posture above.
  2. Gaze goes downward, not very close, to cut down distraction.
  3. Put attention on the out-breath (the exhale). Just go with it.
  4. At the end of each out-breath, relax. You do not have to catch every one.
  5. If the mind wanders, come back to the next out-breath.

What it brings out: the teacher says Trungpa Rinpoche describes the act of placing the mind as the opposite of laziness (being too slack to do anything). You are actually doing something, and that gives freedom from laziness, which she says causes depression.

After the quiet stretch (about 53:48), notice a little relief from daily stress and obligations, and a little uplift. She says the creator of this series calls this joy: a light, quiet joy, not a party-hearty or "I just won the Nobel Prize" joy. It is a bit of freedom of mind, and she notes it is also a fairly ultimate quality. It is the first quality of the warrior being developed here.

You simply come back to your posture, and you are ready for another out-breath.

— SP ch.2 "Discovering Basic Goodness" ¶41

📖 In the books: SP ch.2 "Discovering Basic Goodness" (gaze slightly downward, out-breath, "come back to your posture", labeling thoughts and coming back); GES ch.8 "The King of Basic Goodness" (the breath as a background technique that occupies you); GES ch.7 "Sacredness: Natural Law and Order" and GES ch.1 "A Dot in the Open Sky" (freedom from laziness); GES ch.2 "Working with Early Morning Depression" (the joy of basic goodness)

55:52 Coming back gently, and breathing with the whole body

Meditation brings out a kind of birth of the warrior from within (SP ch.4).

  1. Bring the gaze in quite close, to be really inward.
  2. Come back to the breath. This is "very, very immediate", and it need not be only the out-breath.
  3. At some point you will notice you are not following the breath any more and are engaged in "something else" (a thought).
  4. Come back gently, not judging, to wherever the breath is at that moment.
  5. Later (about 1:01:44), pay attention to both in-breath and out-breath. You can breathe with the whole body, fully embodying yourself. She points out that gas exchange happens at the cellular level in every part of the body.

Two things to know. The breath is always there, and if it is not, you no longer have to worry. And the moment you notice you are somewhere else is a moment of awareness. Celebrate it, but do not linger on it or build "a sand castle" there.

Going inward can be a little or very unsettling. The breath is the anchor, the steadier. We enter this on some faith that basic goodness is worthwhile. The result is that we find we need not be afraid of who we are. The outcome she names is genuineness: no pretending, and kindly being who you are. She calls this "establishing where you live."

The key to warriorship and the first principle of Shambhala vision is not being afraid of who you are. Ultimately, that is the definition of bravery: not being afraid of yourself.

— SP ch.1 "Creating an Enlightened Society" ¶27

📖 In the books: SP ch.2 "Discovering Basic Goodness" (labeling thoughts "thinking" and returning to the breath; being genuine and without deception); GES ch.1 "A Dot in the Open Sky" (include discursive thoughts, keep going back to the breath); GES ch.8 "The King of Basic Goodness" (steady mind, watching thoughts without commentary); SP ch.1 "Creating an Enlightened Society" (not being afraid of who you are); SP ch.4 "Fear and Fearlessness" (the birth of the warrior); gaze closer or lower per instruction: Trungpa, Collected Works Vol. 9 (note on eye gaze); whole-body breathing: Analayo, Mindfulness of Breathing

01:03:06 Opening to the senses

She says opening out to the world happens in steps. The first is the warrior in the world.

  1. Extend the gaze out a little.
  2. Take in what your senses tell you about your surroundings, not your opinion of it.
  3. Follow the breath more lightly. She says attention on the breath is only about 25%, and not to get hung up on the number. The rest goes to the surroundings.
  4. Notice light and shadow, temperature, where you are, smells, sounds, colors.
  5. When the mind wanders (she gives "written our shopping list" as an example), notice that moment as awareness. Come back to exactly where you are, in this specific place and body.

📖 In the books: Trungpa, Mindfulness in Action (only about 25 percent of attention on the breath, a rough estimate); SP ch.5 "Synchronizing Mind and Body" (using sense perceptions, mind and body together); SP ch.18 "How to Rule" and SP ch.21 "The Shambhala Lineage" (the warrior in the world); SP ch.2 "Discovering Basic Goodness" (being "there properly", labeling and coming back)

01:11:47 Relating to the world: gentleness, humility, and extending further

🎥 Worth watching [01:11:47–01:22:20], about 10 min, optional "practice along, audio only": More mostly-silent sitting with a few pointers. Listening with her voice helps, but video is not needed.

Pick up the opening to the world: the technique is being here in this specific time, body and place. - Daring to step into our world is what lets us relate to it. - Relating to the world brings gentleness. - A surprising outcome is humbleness. The world has its own logic and being, and gives us a lot too.

Then:

  1. Raise the gaze and look outward.
  2. Extend yourself further. She reflects that if you extend from here into the neighborhood, the city and beyond, you are doing it in your mind. So it is really extending into the space of mind.
  3. Notice what happens: light, tension on the breath.
  4. When the mind wanders, Trungpa Rinpoche says to come back not to the breath itself but to awareness of breathing. Nothing is negated or turned away from, and you breathe with it.
  5. Let whatever is in your consciousness be. With awareness of breathing, it does not take over the whole landscape. It sits within that larger space.

She asks a question without answering it directly: where did that awareness come from, and where do thoughts come from? Awareness is "just there." The world communicates with us and we with it. Inquisitiveness (what is the world saying, what are we saying to it) goes with this. The outcome is patience with the world, and the effort of simply being present to what is actually going on.

📖 In the books: SP ch.8 "Renunciation and Daring" (a sense of greater space that encompasses any thought); SP ch.5 "Synchronizing Mind and Body" (gentleness); GES ch.5 "Discipline in the Four Seasons" (humility, becoming "very inquisitive"); GES ch.7 "Sacredness: Natural Law and Order" (patience); GES ch.3 "Overcoming Physical Materialism" (daring); awareness of breathing: Trungpa, Mindfulness in Action and Opening the Dharma Treasury

01:20:41 Open sky: dropping technique
  1. Drop any sense of technique.
  2. Look out and in at once. You are aware of where you are and what is going on in your mind, and in the world.
  3. Just sit there and be. She contrasts this with a scientist, who is discovering interesting things in the universe. We just sit and be.
  4. For a minute or two, let the skills you have built (relaxation, honesty, gentleness, patience) flower into simply being.

She says this uncovers our awakeness, which is like the sun.

That awakeness is the dot in space. Inspired or not inspired: both are the dot in space. As long as you come back, that’s fine.

— GES ch.21 "The Great Eastern Sun" ¶649

Part 3 — Symbols, and a poem about the fearful mind (01:36:53–02:20:59) · about 10 min read

01:37:06 Symbols are doors, not definitions

⏭️ Skip [01:36:53–01:37:06]: microphone check and a nod to the group for arriving on time.

The teacher wants to say a few brief things about symbols. The shift to "warrior vision" (a brave, open way of looking) is mostly expressed in symbolic language, rather than in plain explanation.

She gives two examples from the earlier meditation material. - The sun stands for our awakeness, which we are uncovering, not building. - The cocoon stands for everything we wrap ourselves in that keeps us from going anywhere. She adds that some change does happen inside a cocoon. It is a natural process before the butterfly appears, so the cocoon is an analogy, not an insult.

Then she explains how a symbol differs from an analogy. A symbol carries "a whole suitcase full of meanings" (the word she kept reaching for was portmanteau). So a symbol works like a portal: an entry into a whole realm of things. This takes us past our usual conceptual way of thinking.

The sun always shines because you always shine. You are shining at this moment. The sun is a symbol of that ever glowing.

— GES ch.19 "The Mukpo Clan" ¶629

📖 In the books: GES ch.19 "The Mukpo Clan"; GES Prologue "The Kingdom, the Cocoon, the Great Eastern Sun"

Try this: Pick one symbol you already know, such as the sun or a cocoon. Write down three meanings it carries for you, not one definition.

01:39:32 "Warrior": a word that changes meaning

The teacher uses "warrior" because it has already been introduced. For years she struggled with it, because to her a warrior was someone who uses weapons to attack in a war. She calls that the conventional meaning.

Then she offers another one: a warrior is simply someone brave. Think how brave it is to be gentle in certain situations. She is careful to say she doesn't mean "stupid gentle". Sometimes we have to defend ourselves, and some people are dangerous. But it takes real bravery to look at our own minds. That is also warrior.

Her larger point is about how this language works. Inside the Shambhala setting, words begin to take on unconventional meanings, and those meanings open gradually. There's no switch to flip. It's like opening the first page of a book: you don't yet know the whole book, and it unfolds. So she suggests keeping the mind open and letting the notions "flower".

The challenge of warriorship is to step out of the cocoon, to step out into space, by being brave and at the same time gentle.

— SP ch.19 "The Universal Monarch" ¶201

📖 In the books: GES Prologue "The Kingdom, the Cocoon, the Great Eastern Sun"; SP ch.19 "The Universal Monarch"

Try this: Think of a time when being gentle took more courage than being tough. What made it brave?

01:44:00 The whole poem, read aloud

🎥 Worth watching [01:44:00–01:45:03], about 1 min: the teacher reads the whole poem aloud, and the pacing and tone of her reading carry something the printed lines don't.

The poem follows the fearful mind as if it were a child growing up. It starts in a cradle and ends with the mind seeing the Great Eastern Sun. Here is the full text as the group read it.

That mind of fearfulness Should be put in the cradle of loving-kindness And suckled with the profound and brilliant milk of eternal doubtlessness. In the cool shade of fearlessness, Fan it with the fan of joy and happiness. When it grows older, With various displays of phenomena, Lead it to the self-existing playground. When it grows older still, In order to promote the primordial confidence, Lead it to the archery range of the warriors.

— SP ch.10 "Letting Go" ¶113

Then the fearful mind Can change into the warrior’s mind, And that eternally youthful confidence Can expand into space without beginning or end. At that point it sees the Great Eastern Sun.

— SP ch.10 "Letting Go" ¶113

In this stretch the class only covers the first stages (cradle, milk, shade and fan, playground). The remaining stages, the archery range and the final lines, are not reached in this part.

01:45:58 "Put the mind of fearfulness in the cradle of loving-kindness"

A participant reads the first line. The first reactions are warm. - The cradle made one person think of sangha (fellow practitioners). Everyday family and work life may not feel like a cradle, but a community on the same path can be a place to bring fearfulness. - Another found the image beautiful: the tenderness we show babies is a kind of love without judgment. Holding yourself or someone else that way "in your heart" is a lovely picture.

One participant gave the sharpest observation. Their natural reaction to fear is to treat it as unacceptable, to add aggression, to fight it or feel bad about it. Putting it in a cradle is the opposite of that habit. The teacher says this is a good example of the "slightly unconventional" quality: what we do naturally is not what the line asks.
